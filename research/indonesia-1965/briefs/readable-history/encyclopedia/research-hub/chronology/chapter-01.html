Sukarno's formation and anti-colonial politics

该段39项可定位日期记录

1901 6 June 1901: ANRI and Cambridge's biographical summary place Sukarno's birth in Surabaya. They describe his father as Javanese and mother as Balinese. Confidence: high for the broad facts; civil/family records not yet inspected. [S001, S002]

1915 c.1915–early 1920s: ANRI says Sukarno attended school in Surabaya and lodged in H.O.S. Tjokroaminoto's household; it also lists HBS and THS studies. Cambridge identifies the Surabaya nationalist network and marriage to Tjokroaminoto's daughter, Siti Oetari. These are biography/network leads; underlying school, household and correspondence records remain to be inspected. [S001, S002, S093]

1920 1920/1921: Sources disagree on the year Sukarno married Siti Oetari. ANRI's timeline itself records another source's year; Cambridge gives 1921. Do not silently normalize the discrepancy. [S001, S002]

1922 1922/1923: Cambridge dates Sukarno's marriage to Inggit Garnasih to 1922; ANRI's timeline records 24 April 1923 and cites a marriage certificate number. Underlying certificate and divorce records should resolve the conflict. [S001, S002]

1925 1925–1927: Cambridge lists the Bandung General Study Club (1925) and PNI founding (1927); ANRI dates PNI's foundation to 4 July 1927 and names associates. Party founding records, police surveillance and contemporary newspapers remain to be examined. [S001, S002]

1926 November 1926–January 1927: The colonial government's official overview treats the disturbances and uprising as the immediate context for the new mass-internment site; its account presents the policy as protection of public order and prevention of communist agitation. This is the administration's explanation, not a neutral account of each regional revolt. [S496]

1926 18 November 1926 (secondary-source lead): A 2019 thesis says Volksraad government representative Jaap Schrieke proposed placing people selected for internment at one site and that Governor-General De Graeff accepted; the cited Raad minutes have not been read directly. [S505; C867]

1926 27 November and 3 December 1926 (secondary-source lead): The same thesis attributes to Attorney-General H.G.P. Duyfjes correspondence a position that communists and nationalists should not be distinguished as security risks. [S505; C865]

1926 14 December 1926 (secondary-source lead): The thesis says De Graeff considered broad internment partly because he feared many arrestees would be acquitted for lack of evidence; the original letter is not yet read. [S505; C866]

1927 26-27 January 1927: Schoonheyt's 1936 account says the Fomalhout reached a site about 465 km upriver on 26 January, Becking inspected it, and pioneers landed on 27 January to begin clearing. This is a camp-construction voyage, not the first detainee convoy. [S507; C873]

1927 27 January 1927 (later local-government account): Boven Digoel Regency's history page dates the start of the Tanah Merah camp to this day and attributes construction to Captain L. Th. Becking. The page gives no archival citation; confirm against ANRI camp construction records. [S500]

1927 1 February 1927: The official government report says the first transport, 45 internees and 43 family members, left Tanjung Priok for Ambon under arrangements entrusted to the Governor of West Java. It says onward movement to Tanah Merah took place around mid-March when the site was ready. [S496]

1927 10-12 March 1927: ANRI's finding aid describes an instruction about the steamer Formalhout leaving Ambon on 10 March; a separate four-sheet list, reproduced in ANRI's maritime sourcebook, is described as a Semarang group carried by cruiser Java, dated 12 March. These may be separate groups/legs; the latter date is not established as a sailing date. The local-government page also names Segah and Reteh on a Surabaya-Makassar-Ambon route, pending original logs. [S493-S495; S500]

1927 13-14 May 1927: Yamamoto's article reports a Sin Jit Po item about a Surabaya group that arrived at Tandjoeng Perak on 13 May and boarded S.S. Rumphius; the original newspaper issue remains unopened. [S497]

1927 Mid-1927 (Schoonheyt's retrospective account): He says higher authorities abolished the compulsory labor initially planned for internees; the local commandant opposed the change, which was orally conveyed to detainees. The order itself remains missing. [S507; C874]

1927 13 June 1927: Yamamoto summarizes a contemporary newspaper roster of new internees and biographies that include I. Makki/I. Mangki, Noeroet gelar Soetan Radja Endah, Pio, Ishak and Mohamad Sjahmin gelar Pandji Nagara. Verify spellings and organizational details against the issue and individual registers. [S497]

1927 1 November 1927: The official camp table prints 535 men and 13 women internees but a total of 538; it prints family components of 2, 236 and 194 but a family total of 382, and grand total 930. Component arithmetic instead gives 980. The correct November total is unresolved. [S496; C852]

1928 1 February 1928: The same official report's camp-population column reconciles to 1,139 residents, including 666 internees and 473 family members; this is a different denominator from the legal count of persons subject to the measure. [S496; C852]

1928 1 April 1928: The government report says 823 people were subject to the exorbitant measure: 383 former officials/employees, 79 farmers, 361 other occupations; regional split 692 Java/Madura and 131 outer territories. [S496; C850]

1928 7 June 1928: In the Dutch House debate, Cramer and de Visser criticized or challenged the policy and camp governance; speakers disputed Article 37's scope, labor pressure and calls for release. These are parliamentary positions, not a judicial finding. [S503]

1929 1929-1931: Sukarno was arrested, tried and imprisoned, then released in 1931; Cambridge/ANRI biographical records summarize these milestones, while court and prison files remain to be checked. [S001-S002]

1930 April-June 1930: W.P. Hillen investigated conditions at Boven Digoel. The 1931 official report says the review led to immediate revocation for 219 people and continued observation of categories defined by work willingness and perceived political attitude; a 21 October 1930 parliamentary debate refers to 412 proposed releases and contests both the review and official interpretation. The original Hillen report is cataloged by ANRI as Binnenlandsbestuur no.1308, 37 sheets, but not yet read. [S495; S502; S504]

1930 April and 22 July 1930 (dates kept distinct): Schoonheyt says Hillen visited in April; the 2019 thesis's prose calls his report a March document but its footnote identifies a report dated 22 July, while the 1931 annual report dates the inquiry to April-June. Read the original no.1308 report before resolving fieldwork and report dates. [S493; S502; S505; S507; C869-C870]

1930 10-15 October 1930: According to the 2019 thesis, De Graeff and Colonial Affairs Minister De Graaff exchanged letters about a committee to review internment procedures; the thesis says De Graeff appointed one despite the minister's concern about political consequences. The letters and committee report are still missing. [S505; C871-C872]

1931 1 April 1931: The government report gives 1,678 camp residents: 967 internees, 268 spouses, 397 children and 46 other family members. It says 516 people left in Q1 1931, chiefly after release decisions; the count includes a different population snapshot and must not be added to later releases without registers. [S502]

1931 18 June 1931: The government report says it lifted internment for a second 186-person group, the B-group. Adding this to the 219 immediate cases yields 405, seven fewer than the 412 cited in the 1930 parliamentary debate; definitions or implementation dates may differ. [S502; S504; C862]

1931 1931: A later labor-history article cites an estimate of about 100,000 financial members of urban unions, compared with around 20,000 members in nationalist parties. The comparison signals the scale attributed to labor networks but is not an independently checked membership census. [S845; C1501]

1933 1933: Cambridge/ANRI summaries say Sukarno was rearrested and sent into exile to Flores; exact police, prison and exile orders remain to be retrieved. [S001-S002]

1933 30 August 1933: Yamamoto reports that Sukarno wrote a confidential letter after rearrest, asking that proceedings/internment stop and offering to cease political activity; the original letter has not been read, and the claim that he feared Digoel is the article's interpretation. His actual exile was to Flores, later Bengkulu. [S001-S002; S497]

1933 1933 (Schoonheyt's account): He says harbor workers and bribed sailors exchanged uncensored letters/periodicals, while a government steamer captain, at the local administrator's request, intercepted several smuggled letters during one 1933 inspection. This is a reported event, not proof of continuous mail censorship. [S507; C877]

1934 15 and 29 December 1934: The 1938 proceedings describe an order to fence residential yards on 15 December, followed by a petition signed by 45 Tanah Tinggi internees on 29 December. [S506; C879-C880]

1935 7-27 March 1935: Sjahrir's published letters from Tanah Merah describe the divided administrative/exile settlement, paid work and everyday scarcity, as well as his perception that work and official ideas of political reform affected release prospects. This is a firsthand individual account for 1935, not a 1927 transport witness or a camp census. [S499] [S001, S002]

1935 24 July 1935 and 21 August 1937: The proceedings cite government replies to that petition on these dates; the letters themselves have not been retrieved. [S506; C879]

1938 5 January 1938: At the 16th College of Delegates meeting, Thamrin presented the petition committee's second report; his request to send relevant papers to the government and petitioners was added, and the conclusion passed without roll-call voting. [S506; C881-C883]

1938 1938: Sukarno was transferred from Flores to Bengkulu, according to the available biographical summaries; the transfer order and correspondence remain to be found. [S001-S002]

1920 1920s–1942: Wider nationalist, socialist, Islamic, communist and labor networks, as well as Sukarno's intellectual influences and family/private relations, remain only partially covered. (Coverage CV002–CV003, CV020.)

1954 25–31 March 1954 (congress statutes, OCR text): Gerwis's second congress in Jakarta approved the Gerwani statutes. Article 15 describes a national–province–regency/city–district–village/workplace structure, including cells of at least five nearby members; Article 28 establishes functional sections for women's rights, children's welfare, organization, education and treasury. Article 62 specifies a Rp1 entry fee and Rp0.25 dues, and Article 63 allocates 90% of entry fees/dues/other monthly revenue to regional and lower bodies and 10% to the central leadership. This is a prescribed constitutional financing and organization model, not audited evidence of collections, functioning branches or actual transfers. Source: 1954 congress souvenir-book OCR, https://pustaka.taratsa.id/read/618/ocr/pdf/ , especially Articles 15, 28, 62–63. Original page images not yet compared; formal S/C registration pending full ledger deduplication.

1951 6–8 January 1951 (legal enactment, distinct from implementation): Indonesian Law No. 1/1951, enacted 6 January and promulgated 8 January, extended the Republic's 1948 Law No. 12 on work nationally. Its Article II provided for phased commencement through government regulations by sector and provision. Thus enactment does not establish that each menstrual, maternity, nursing or night-work provision was already implemented in all workplaces in 1954. Official BPK legal metadata https://peraturan.bpk.go.id/Details/39807/uu-no-1-tahun-1951 and statutory transcription https://peraturan.info/uu/1951/1/isi ; verify original PDF and implementing regulations. Formal S/C registration pending full ledger deduplication.

1951 8 January 1951 (implementing regulation, distinct from workplace compliance): Government Regulation PP 4/1951 (dated 6 January, promulgated 8 January; LN 1951/7) extended earlier PP 7/1948 as amended and PP 13/1950. Pasal I reproduces implementation of 1948 Labor Law Article 13(1)–(4) on menstrual, maternity and nursing provisions; Pasal III provides that these protections entered into force upon promulgation in Jakarta, former East Indonesia, East Sumatra and West Kalimantan. The earlier 1948 effective date in the incorporated provisions applies to the former Republic territory, not automatically to all post-1950 territories. These are legal commencement rules, not evidence of compliance. Modern text transcriptions opened: https://peraturan.info/pp/1951/4/isi and https://pasal.id/peraturan/pp/pp-no-4-tahun-1951 ; official LN scan and 1951 correction still to inspect. Formal S/C IDs pending full ledger deduplication.

Japanese occupation and independence

该段4项可定位日期记录

1942 1942: Japanese authorities released Sukarno from Dutch exile/internment and used him in occupation-era political bodies. Cambridge confirms his leadership in Japanese-sponsored bodies; the exact institutions and dates require Japanese and Indonesian records. [S001]

1942 1942–1945: Sukarno's participation in occupation-sponsored organizations and mass mobilization formed part of both nationalist politics and Japan's war economy. The position involved agency and political responsibility while operating under coercive occupation; the extent and consequences must be investigated rather than assumed. Romusha labor, famine, PETA and recruitment into Japanese structures are major research lines. [S001, S016, S017]

1944 1944–1945: Later historiography debates Java famine mortality and how postwar claims, public memory and political actors represented it. Estimates have incentives and uncertain data; do not lift headline totals without methods. [S016]

1945 17 August 1945: Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed independence two days after Japan's surrender. The exact timing, youth pressure, Japanese restrictions and drafting of the proclamation require comparison of the text, BPUPKI/PPKI minutes, Japanese records, contemporary press and recollections. The Indonesian State Secretariat page was surfaced but not fully extractable in this pass. [S001, S006]

Revolution and the Madiun conflict

该段14项可定位日期记录

1945 1945–1946: British forces under Allied authority, rather than U.S. forces, took responsibility for Japanese surrender in most of Indonesia and faced the return of Dutch forces. A U.S. retrospective describes this command arrangement and British contact with Republican officials; British, Dutch and Indonesian records are still needed. [S012]

1945 19 September–15 October 1945: Tedjasukmana describes a 19 September workers’ meeting and cites Sari (1950) for 15 October as BBI’s founding date, while later summaries commonly call 19 September the foundation. Treat the meeting date and formal founding date as unresolved distinct claims. The same account describes the creation of the Partai Buruh Indonesia while BBI continued as a union organization. [S845; S846; C1500; C1505]

1946 4–15 January 1946: The Persatuan Perdjuangan emerged from a broad coalition that demanded full independence before negotiations; at its 15 January congress the minimum programme was modified to keep room for diplomacy. The coalition included parties and armed organizations with differing positions and was not simply another name for the PKI. [S847; C1506]

1946 27 June–6 July 1946: Sjahrir was detained by A.K. Jusuf using a warrant authorized by Sudarsono and processed through local military and police channels. Sukarno signed Decree No. 1 on 29 June to assume full government power temporarily; Sjahrir returned on 1 July. On 3 July, Sudarsono’s group brought Yamin’s proposed decrees to the president, seeking a new cabinet and a transfer of defense and political-economic authority. Sukarno rejected the proposals. Later testimony about Sudirman’s instructions conflicts with his 1948 affidavit. The government’s 6 July statement blamed Tan Malaka’s group; later authors dispute Tan Malaka’s direct operational role. The ANRI catalogue identifies original trial proceedings and the 1948 judgment, neither of which has yet been read in full. [S847–S849; C1507–C1511]

1946 1946–1947: U.S. records describe recognition of the Republic's de facto authority under the Linggadjati framework, participation in mediation, and attempts to prevent a breakdown. These records are the U.S. government's account of its own policy. [S012]

1946 May–November 1946; 1947: The labor histories describe competing federation models GASBI and GSBV and SOBSI’s formation on 29 November 1946. SOBSI’s first congress affiliated it with the World Federation of Trade Unions; Hearman describes close ties with the PKI while preserving SOBSI’s separate organizational identity. [S845; S846; C1500; C1503]

1947 1947–1948: The United States worked through cease-fire, Good Offices Committee and Renville processes; it also discussed possible economic assistance to an interim arrangement. Policy mixed mediation, economic interests, anti-communism and alliance concerns. [S012]

1948 26 May 1948: The UN Committee of Good Offices reported a negotiated conference structure: Steering Committee, four policy committees and coordinated reports to the Security Council with comments from the Republic and Netherlands. Its conference rules also allowed private, unrecorded informal discussions and confidential working papers. [S844; C1498–C1499]

1948 3 September 1948: Before the Madiun events, U.S. State Department analyst James W. Barco told Dean Rusk that a settlement should address what he called a communist threat while preserving Dutch and U.S. strategic and economic interests. This documents U.S. policy thinking, not an operation in Indonesia. [S843; C1497]

1948 18–20 September 1948: U.S. Consul General Livengood's cable relayed Sukarno's radio broadcast through a listener, ANETA information, a Republican communiqué and statements by Republican leaders. A second cable reproduced the Republic's account of an armed seizure and Information Minister's claim that events seemed part of a Moscow plan. These are distinct attributed reports, not independent U.S. observation or proof of Soviet operational control. [S007, S008; C145–C146]

1948 September–December 1948: Republican forces suppressed the Madiun movement; leading figures including Musso and Amir Sjarifuddin were killed or captured, according to later scholarly summaries. Exact sequence, command responsibility, casualty counts, executions and political intention remain to be reconstructed from Indonesian, military, party, Dutch and regional records. [S010, S015]

时期未定 Interpretation remains contested: Scholarship has challenged both a simple foreign-directed-coup account and a purely spontaneous/domestic account. Army reorganization, factional rivalry, breakdown of negotiations and Musso's return all appear in the literature. [S013–S015, S058]

1948 18 December 1948–1949: After the Dutch second military action, U.S. officials advocated Security Council pressure and used the prospect of economic aid to the Netherlands as leverage; an official later retrospective says U.S. support shifted toward Indonesian sovereignty in part because of Cold War calculations and Dutch military action. This sequence complicates a one-line account of U.S. policy. [S011, S012]

1949 27 December 1949: U.S. retrospective records the Round Table Conference agreement as the basis for transfer of sovereignty. Dutch, Indonesian, UN and federal-state perspectives must be added. [S012]

Parliamentary state, Guided Democracy and foreign conflict

该段58项可定位日期记录

1950 1950–1957: Parliamentary governments, regional rebellions, the 1955 elections, Sukarno's personal authority, army political/economic power and party growth need a dedicated chronological tranche. The current archive has incomplete official election leads; the new U.S. primary files add evidence of aid and influence strategies, not a full Indonesian-side chronology. (Coverage CV007.)

1952 5 Jan 1952–12 Jan 1953: A U.S. memorandum later recorded that military/economic aid assurances under Section 511(a) triggered suspicions of a foreign-policy compromise and contributed to the Sukiman Cabinet's fall; the Wilopo government negotiated a replacement under Section 511(b) that continued economic grants but discontinued grant military aid. This is the U.S. account of a domestic political controversy; Indonesian records are needed for a fuller causal history. [S094]

1953 Aug–Nov 1953: U.S. policy documents described intended influence through personal contacts with Indonesian leaders, especially Sukarno; trade/market support for rubber and tin; economic/technical assistance; possible military training/equipment for internal security; information and exchange programs; and stronger intelligence collection. The documents show approved intent and channels, not a uniform record of execution. [S094–S095]

1955 1955 elections: Search-indexed KPU results distinguish the DPR election from the Constituent Assembly election; the PKI totals differ slightly. These numbers remain provisional until printed official result tables are checked. [S035]

1955 1955: A later labor-history synthesis, citing a 1982 study, reports that the Ministry of Labour listed more than 1,500 national, regional and local unions, about half unaffiliated with a federation. This is a union-organization count, not a worker-membership total; the original ministry directory remains to be located. [S845; C1502]

1957 12 Nov 1957: A State memorandum describing an interagency discussion with CIA, Defense and military services reported U.S. officer training and argued that arms sales could strengthen pro-U.S. officers and Army orientation. It also discussed small or selectively timed shipments and concerns over use against the regional rebellion or West New Guinea; its recommendations should not be read as proof of a completed shipment. [S096]

1957 1957–1958: U.S. official records acknowledge covert support for regional dissidents, including approved political action, military assistance and air support; CAT pilots volunteered and flew combat missions. A separate April 1958 U.S.–UK memo discusses options, including covert-only action and hypothetical intervention. Documented activity and merely discussed options must remain separate. [S018–S020]

1959 5 July 1959: Decree No. 150 dissolved the Constituent Assembly and restored the 1945 Constitution. The text is primary legal evidence for what the decree ordered, not a complete answer to its legitimacy or political effects. [S022, S036]

1958 1958–1965: Dutch enterprise nationalization, Indonesian-Soviet military and economic ties, Chinese-Indonesian relations, Japanese reparations, U.S. policy and British/Australian regional operations form separate foreign-intervention strands. Evidence levels are highly uneven; see the intervention matrix. [S045–S051, S056]

1960 1960: Indonesia enacted a Basic Agrarian Law and a sharecropping law; land ceilings were also legislated. Their implementation, uneven enforcement, local land conflict and party/union actions are not inferable from enactment alone. [S037–S039, S041]

1960 1960–1965: PKI and affiliated mass organizations expanded, but membership estimates are not census counts and each organization had distinct membership, autonomy and local relations. Need an Indonesian party/union archive and regional case studies. [S040, S053]

1962 19 February 1962: Keppres 48 established a nationwide non-military civil-defense organization within the national-defense system and assigned central planning to the Minister of National Security; it was made retroactive to 19 December 1961. This is general legal architecture, not evidence of a specific 1965 course. [S1443; C2717]

1962 4 April 1962: Keppres 128 transferred central civil-defense and popular-resistance planning/coordination to the First Deputy Minister for Defense/Security, retroactive to 6 March. [S1444; C2718]

1962 6 September 1962: UU 14 enacted the general-mobilization framework retroactively from 19 December 1961; it sets a presidential declaration/call-up mechanism and general training duties for eligible citizens. No 1965 declaration, call-up, or Lubang Buaya-specific authorization has yet been found. [S1445; C2719–C2721]

1962 20 July 1962: An interagency police-assistance report frames police aid as a U.S. counter-subversion and counterinsurgency tool, recommends stronger AID management and training, and assigns AID normal funding/operation except covert aspects or programs assigned to Defense. This is a global program design, not proof of covert Indonesian action. [S622; C1040]

1962 6–7 December 1962: A progress report says AID's Office of Public Safety and interagency group had been set up; the International Police Academy and quicker equipment delivery had not yet been initiated as of the December 6 review. [S623; C1041]

1963 January–August 1963: AID reported that Indonesia's Army civic-action program received $670,000 in equipment and that U.S.-university training plans were developed for senior officers serving in key government jobs. These are agency-reported implementation statements; itemized receipts and rosters remain absent. [S624; C1042]

1963 22 August 1963: The same AID report described a global public-safety apparatus spanning 31 countries, with 247 AID personnel and Defense officers assigned to AID; a joint AID/Defense film taught complementary police/military counterinsurgency roles. [S624; C1043]

1963 16 December 1963: Public Law 88-205 added section 620(j), conditioning assistance to Indonesia under the Act on a presidential determination of essentiality to U.S. national interest and requiring current congressional reporting. [S638; C1044]

1964 6–7 January 1964: Rusk recommended a reduced program and described aid as preserving U.S. influence and strengthening anti-Communist elements; Bundy's memorandum recorded an 80 percent reduction in planned FY64 assistance and argued both sides of a full cutoff. Rusk's proposed $15 million program and $29.5 million pipeline were not final disbursement figures. [S625–S628; C1045–C1046]

1964 9 January 1964: A secret Australian cable summarizes Subandrio's message to K. C. O. Shann: Indonesia wanted a way out but would not accept an imposed settlement; Shann says Subandrio would not commit on Borneo border operations. This is Australian diplomatic reporting, not Indonesian minutes. [S660; C1101]

1964 14 January 1964: McGeorge Bundy's instruction to Robert Kennedy linked continuation of some U.S. aid to Sukarno assurances of a cease-fire and negotiation; it cited a statutory cutoff for aggression. Editorial notes identify revisions, omitted sentences and uncertain transmission channel, so the text establishes internal U.S. instructions rather than Kennedy's exact spoken message. [S655; C1089–C1091]

1964 17–23 January 1964: U.S. embassy cables from Tokyo, Jakarta and Manila and Rusk's internal message report Sukarno's conditional cease-fire position, different implementation times for regular troops and guerrillas, the proposed Macapagal-Lopez-Thailand timetable, British concerns about withdrawal implications, and Washington's view of aid as leverage. These are U.S.-side diplomatic reports and instructions, not joint minutes or proof of field compliance; Kennedy declined to equate Indonesian guerrilla operations with Malaysian/British defensive activity. [S656–S658, S663–S664; C1092–C1098, C1106–C1111]

1964 28 January 1964: Australian Cabinet approved a £3 million special fund for defence assistance to Malaysia and a mission to specify requests; it wanted aid identifiable as Australian but quiet and not advertised as dramatic intervention. Approval and public-information design do not establish funds spent or items delivered. [S661; C1102–C1103]

1964 3 February 1964: NSAM 278 records the President's decision to defer a determination until regional talks concluded and to continue existing economic and MAP programs under State and Defense review. [S629; C1047]

1964 31 January 1964: The State Department instructed its ambassadors to coordinate a common U.S. position. Its stated minimum settlement conditions included ending Indonesian incursions, preserving Malaysia's sovereignty and Western alliance orientation, and retaining sufficient Western military presence; it allowed only a pro forma Sabah/Sarawak popular-will test that left their legal status intact. These were U.S. policy boundaries, not terms accepted by the parties. [S665; C1112–C1114]

1964 18 February 1964: Washington urged Malaysia to keep negotiating while telling Jakarta that guerrilla withdrawal was not a bargaining concession and could be handled quietly to protect Indonesian prestige. The cable's count of 150–200 Indonesians among roughly 2,000 local participants is a U.S. estimate, not an independently verified count. Washington said talks were necessary to preserve the cease-fire and Malaysia's international position. [S666; C1115–C1118]

1964 19 February 1964: NSC staff member Michael Forrestal proposed using Macapagal's visit to press Sukarno and encouraging Tunku to narrow his withdrawal demand to regular Indonesian personnel. The memo also reports a quiet British capture of twelve marines, which needs British and Indonesian corroboration. [S667; C1119–C1122]

1964 29 February 1964: Before the next Bangkok meeting, the State Department told Jakarta to press Subandrio on guerrilla withdrawal, told Kuala Lumpur to hear Indonesian proposals, and urged Thai observers to investigate incidents on Malaysian territory only. Its footnote relays a U.S. Embassy assessment of broad Indonesian support for confrontation and limited conventional preparations. [S668; C1123–C1126]

1964 3–6 March 1964: State proposed an 'educational' outreach through U.S. military relationships to Nasution and other officers to influence policy against confrontation, while avoiding the appearance of a campaign against Sukarno. A later editorial note reports that Nasution did not respond to hints of U.S. support for a crisis, but the cited cable has not been opened. [S669; C1127–C1131]

1964 4 March 1964: A White House staff record shows Bundy pressing toward an aid cutoff while Komer expected Kennedy to criticize Tunku too; this was a staff discussion, not the President's final decision. [S670; C1132–C1133]

1964 5 March 1964: Forrestal's memorandum reported that the second Bangkok ministerial meeting broke down over guerrilla withdrawal and that Malaysia declared the cease-fire no longer operative. He also relayed a request for Australian assessment of escalation and argued against U.S. involvement in a wider conflict. [S671; C1134–C1137]

1964 11 March 1964: Robert Komer's secret memorandum describes a gradual-disengagement / resumed-talks / later-summit formula and recommends quietly signaling U.S. displeasure while avoiding escalation. This is an NSC staff assessment and recommendation, not proof the message was delivered or influenced Sukarno. [S659; C1099–C1100]

1964 12 March 1964: Ambassador Jones said months of U.S. arguments had not perceptibly slowed confrontation; his cable assessed Sukarno as directing policy and the military as seeking to outcompete the PKI while avoiding full war. He also described senior officers as insulated from ordinary economic hardship, an attributed U.S. class analysis. [S672; C1138–C1142]

1964 13 March 1964: Rusk told Britain's Butler that U.S. aid had been reduced mainly to training, which Washington viewed as investment in the next generation of officers, and warned that all support could end while invoking ANZUS. [S673; C1143–C1144]

1964 17–19 March 1964: Washington proposed managing publicity around Sukarno's conditional acceptance of the Lopez formula; Nasution asked to preserve U.S. military training/civic-action ties and said the Army executed government decisions, controlled Indonesian volunteers but not all guerrillas, and maintained quiet contact with Malaysian officers. These are U.S.-reported conversations, not verbatim transcripts. [S674–S675; C1145–C1152]

1964 10 April 1964: Sukarno gave Ambassador Jones his definition of the cease-fire, restated his Sabah/Sarawak position and alleged British/Malaysian actions; the allegations require independent checking. [S676; C1153–C1157]

1964 14–17 April 1964: State asked ambassadors to compare disengagement, expanded mediation, use of remaining U.S. leverage and possible Dutch involvement. An uncleared Manila summary records concerns over observers, UN use, British cross-border action and ANZUS escalation; its speculation about British intent is not evidence of British policy. [S677–S678; C1158–C1166]

1964 January–May 1964 (reported on 12 May): Australia’s internal account says Robert Kennedy’s January visit and a Borneo ceasefire preceded February/March Bangkok ministerial talks; it reports U.S. efforts to secure a three-part disengagement/talks/summit proposal and an Indonesian–Malaysian disagreement about the timing of troop withdrawal. This is Australia’s relay of negotiations, not the meeting minutes. [S642; C1072]

1964 14 April 1964: Australian Cabinet deferred an immediate response to Britain’s request for a strategic-reserve battalion and SAS in Borneo pending Australian assessment and a check of the U.S. view; it separately made engineering, naval, helicopter and transport support available or offered. [S640; C1059–C1060]

1964 28 April 1964: Australia’s Defence Committee considered GCHQ’s request for 201 Squadron personnel to provide tactical SIGINT. It first sought replacement/additional British personnel to avoid leaving Singapore short; deployment of some Australian operators was conditional. [S643; C1064–C1066]

1964 14–29 April 1964: Assistant Secretary Frederick G. Dutton's letter, printed in the Senate Record, reported about 280 Indonesian military and police trainees in the United States, 51 of them police, and outlined police, civic-action and career military courses. It said seven former Fort Bragg trainees had moved elsewhere and that their course was counterinsurgency, not guerrilla warfare. Senator Kenneth B. Keating described the course as guerrilla warfare and argued against the program; his framing is a political claim, distinct from Dutton's letter. [S630; C1048–C1049]

1964 8–15 May 1964: Paltridge’s submission reports that the Australian JIC moderated the British threat estimate and recommends refusing the battalion/SAS request at that stage; the FAD Committee accepted this on 12 May. The same submission relays Britain’s limited hot-pursuit/counter-fire authorization, but the cited British messages are not published. [S641; C1061–C1063]

1964 13 May 1964: DSD’s classified operating signal assigned 201 Squadron operational responsibility for Borneo SIGINT with British/Australian resources, Labuan and Kuching stations, DSD-managed tasking/distribution, an agreed cover story and controlled reconnaissance/communications procedures. ASD later says operators were at Labuan by June. [S644–S646; C1067–C1071]

1964 29 June 1964: Rusk's memorandum and attached State Department rationale recommend continuing selected AID and MAP programs; they describe police training/equipment and military training as instruments for U.S. ties and influence. The detailed program tabs are not printed, and the d52 copy has no presidential approval mark. [S620; C1035]

1964 3 July 1964: Army General Staff Instruction 11/64 records approval for one 201 Signal Squadron troop (1 officer, 37 other ranks), with the main troop at Labuan and a small Kuching detachment; it specifies command, three-month rotations, British transport/logistics, need-to-know handling and no publicity. This is authorization and administrative design, not a personnel roster or proof every arrangement was implemented. [S654; C1082–C1084]

1964 17–20 August 1964: After the Senate passed the Tower aid amendment, a State telegram records Johnson asking George Ball to seek its elimination or make it discretionary; Marshall Green then proposed a quiet discussion with Sukarno and Subandrio about ending military aid before the bill became law. This proposal is not evidence the discussion occurred. On August 20, CIA analysts issued an attributed interpretation of Sukarno's Independence Day speech. [S631–S633; C1050–C1052]

1964 31 August 1964: A separate Rusk/Bundy memorandum records checked presidential approval of deferring major military communications equipment (including a funded item of about $8 million) and withholding military-type supplies to the National Police, including the Mobile Brigade, while continuing nonmilitary police training and non-sensitive equipment to preserve U.S. influence. Approval is documented; delivery and effect are not. [S621; C1035]

1965 8 January 1965: Senator John Tower introduced S.328 to prohibit further aid and U.S.-government-facility training for Indonesian military and police personnel; it was referred to the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and was not an enacted law. Tower's account of the 1964 vote and conference process needs the original roll-call and conference records. [S634; C1053]

1965 27 January 1965: The Australian Cabinet accepted a response to Malaysian force requests subject to logistics and prior U.S./New Zealand consultation; its recorded political concern included accusations of Australian aggression if 3RAR took part in cross-border operations. [S647; C1073–C1074]

1964 1964–1965 (retrospective biography): Sidarto Danusubroto's BPK and Wantimpres profiles list U.S. police/security courses, including Washington, Norfolk and Fort Bragg. Their course sequencing differs; no personnel roster or USAID project record was retrieved. [S618–S619; C1034]

1964 1964–1965: A February 1965 U.S. 303 Committee progress report describes political action against the PKI, propaganda and liaison/support; some activities were underway while an additional program awaited approval. The agency is partly redacted. [S024]

1965 22 March 1965: The AWM 3RAR unit record dates the battalion’s formal takeover from the 1/7 Gurkhas in Sarawak to this day; it later records 32 Claret operations and a 27 May contact. [S649; C1075]

1965 1 May 1965: The 3RAR Operations Room log recorded a report of a possible attack on Stass and Serikin around 5 May; this records a warning received by the unit, not proof of Indonesian intent or an attack. [S648; C1078]

1965 10 May 1965: A 3RAR intelligence report dated 13 May records about 60 traders from Babang, Kindau and Risau at a border post, secondhand claims of TNI following them and a letter demanding relocation of the post or an end to trade; traders reportedly said TNI claimed the pepper brought there and prevented them selling their own. These are Australian unit intelligence claims; neither letter nor trader testimony is independently available here. [S648; C1088]

1965 13 May 1965: 3RAR INTREP No.6, “The Attack Which Never Came,” says two parties of about 150 TNI each left for an intended 5 May attack on Serikin, then returned on 9 May without reaching the border. The report’s commentary says the unit had deployed a company and speculates that increased harassing fire may have deterred the parties. This is a report of intelligence received and the unit’s interpretation, not independent proof of force size, intent or cause. [S648; C1086–C1087]

1965 27 May 1965: AWM’s unit summary dates a 3RAR ambush/contact with Indonesian forces at Sungei Koemba to this date. Separately, the contemporaneous 3RAR SITREP for 26–27 May records enemy mortar fire near the border, assessed as probably 81-mm; that is the unit’s attribution and estimate, not independent identification. [S648–S649; C1075; C1085]

1965 20 July 1965: A U.S. record discusses a commercial license for communications equipment requested by the Indonesian Army; licensing is not proof of delivery or use. [S025]

30 September Movement, state violence and transfer of power

1965 10 March–28 September 1965 (reported AURI/Hansip training chain): A 1994 State Secretariat history, consulted through an unverified blog transcription, claims an Omar Dhani instruction and coded Korud radiograms, failed base plans after Koramil reactions, and later calls of about 2,000 trainees in four battalions. Sujono’s 16 February 1966 military-court testimony instead says he initiated the course under earlier defense instructions, described a sudden 28 September group of about 1,500, and in cross-examination said he had only a report of “1,000-something”; he said the trainees were not a formally constituted AURI Hansip unit and cited 100 Garand from Suradi. The Rawabinong 200/26 special-course counts are separate. These are source-attributed statements, not a reconciled roster or proof that training caused the movement. [S1353;S1447–S1451;C2722–C2741]

1965 30 September–1 October 1965: A U.S. intelligence document dated 6 October identifies Untung, records Suharto as Kostrad commander assuming Army command, and advances an early leftist-coup hypothesis. It is evidence of what U.S. analysts believed at that date, not proof of PKI command or Sukarno's responsibility. [S026]

1965 Before 30 September 1965, PKI armed-line as described in later oral history: Liem says the party had no plan for a party-led armed struggle and worked inside the existing political order; after the ban and repression, Sudisman shifted surviving cadres toward armed resistance during 1966. Distinguish this reconstruction from Fifth Force proposals, volunteer training and specific military experience. [S1317;S261;C2346]

1965 1965 G30S decision evidence (participant records read later): Supardjo’s 1966 postmortem describes three leadership groups (Aidit, Sjam and Untung) and criticizes the “firecracker” strategy of expecting a Jakarta action to trigger regional and popular support. Roosa’s account of Iskandar Subekti’s 1986 analysis places Aidit and Sjam at the center of planning but does not establish a formal Central Committee resolution; the original PKI minutes and Supardjo manuscript remain unlocated. [S252; C2314–C2318]

1965 1965 G30S responsibility, additional retrospective account: In a 2000 interview published by André Liem, Rewang said the PKI as an institution had not decided to launch the movement and recalled Politburo members as confused afterward. This differs in vantage point from Supardjo and Subekti; it is not a contemporaneous record and does not prove that no individual leaders knew. [S1317; C2320]

1965 October 1965: One U.S. memorandum's statement that U.S. involvement had been limited to quiet assurances of non-interference is narrowly scoped; the same documentary record documents earlier covert action and later assistance. [S023–S027]

1965 10–13 October 1965, East Java: Leksana’s reading of Brawijaya records reports that Panca Tunggal dismissed a 2,000-person religious demonstration in Pasuruan on 10 October; on 11 October Zeni Battalion 5 was instructed to work with Puterpra to control demonstrations and prevent destruction of property; a Pupelrada was established under Korem 083 in Malang on 13 October. These are regional orders and reports, not proof of uniform execution. [S295;C2569;C2573]

1965 21 October 1965, East Java: The Pancasila Operation order attributed to Basuki Rachmat required six-hour situation reports to Surabaya and allowed pro-Army civilian forces to assist; Leksana says copies were sent to Korem 081–083 and other command recipients. [S295;C2331]

1965 26 October–27 November 1965, East Java: Radiograms placed pro-Army organizations under Puterpra, authorized combative training and investigation teams, ordered Puterpra armed, assigned civilian auxiliaries resident-registration and operation tasks, and later called for mass movements to be stopped and directed through Hansip. The article cites T.702/1965 for both 23 and 27 November instructions; the serial/date conflict remains unresolved. [S295;C2332–C2333;C2570–C2571]

1965 26 October 1965, Central Java: Hammer reports a Solo meeting chaired by Sarwo Edhie deciding to use pre-existing village guards and add newly recruited auxiliaries, with brief RPKAD training. This is a Central Java deployment account, not a uniform national template. [S139;C2572]

1965 14 October 1965, East Java: Leksana cites Radiogram T.587/1965 instructing Kodim to investigate Gerwani and Pemuda Rakyat members for information on G30S; whether this order directly influenced killings is unclear. [S295;S1410;C2586]

1965 16 November 1965, Korem 083: A military report recorded four killing cases, one with four bodies found in a rice field; the report often left the killers unidentified. This is the army report’s wording, not an established identification of perpetrators. [S1410;C2580]

1965 19–29 November 1965, South Malang: A local Pancasila Operation order was dated 19 November; Leksana says the operation under Captain Hasan Basri began on 29 November in Donomulyo, over five weeks after the East Java-wide 21 October operation order. [S1410;C2582]

1965 30 November 1965: A separate T.715/1965 radiogram reportedly required district military commanders to accept the “self-dissolution” of left organizations and to witness it with Panca Tunggal/National Front representatives. The approval rule complicates an unqualified claim that dissolutions were voluntary. [S1410;C2579]

1965 November–December 1965, Korem 083 detention log: Leksana reproduces changing daily totals, including 2,472 on 4 November, 6,259 at noon on 12 December and 4,431 on 29 December. The log includes an unexplained 1,509 on 23 November and blank totals on 4 and 23 December; these are detention counts, not deaths. [S1410;C2577–C2578]

1965 4 December 1965, Korem 083: A report to the Brawijaya commander said PKI in the Korem area had been “terminated.” This is a military status claim, not a death total; the South Malang operation continued into January 1966. [S1410;C2581–C2582]

1965 November 1965–June 1966: U.S. documents record a covert procurement path for medical supplies and a communications system for anti-communist Army leaders. A follow-up reports partial assembly/use, not delivery of the entire network. Requests, approvals, purchases, shipments, receipt and effect require separate evidence. [S029–S033]

1965 December 1965 onward: U.S. records document transfers of lists of suspected PKI and affiliated-organization members to Indonesian authorities. This establishes transfer, not the causal role of any particular name in a detention or killing. [S032]

1965 1965–1966 killings and mass arrests: Cribb’s full 2002 review describes estimates from 100,000 to two million and warns that individual evidence and demographic methods did not permit a precise national count; the commonly used several-hundred-thousand estimate remains approximate. Woolgar reports a secondary estimate of about 2,000 ethnic-Chinese victims against about 500,000 total, which yields only a rough derived ratio (about 0.4%), not a measured national ethnic proportion. No victim-level national ethnicity dataset was found in this pass; local patterns and later anti-Chinese discrimination varied by region. [S1311;S231;C2308–C2313] Ananta et al. report that the 1961 census did not collect ethnicity and the next postcolonial ethnicity census was 2000; this reinforces why later population tables cannot calculate victim shares. Cribb and Coppel’s approximately 2,000 Chinese-death estimate remains a scholarly estimate without a national victim roster. [S1318;S1322;C2340–C2341]

1966 11 March 1966–1967: Supersemar document versions, chain of custody, subsequent orders and the legal transfer of authority remain unresolved here. The MPR database returned 403 and a repository PDF timed out. No authenticity verdict has been entered. [S054, S055]

1967 6 February 1967: BPK and Wantimpres profiles date Sidarto's presidential-aide service to 1967–1968; Wantimpres gives 6 February 1967–17 March 1968. His 2024 oral-history account gives a less precise 1966–1968 range and a personal explanation for his appointment; no signed appointment order has been read. [S616; S618–S619; C1036]

1967 September 1967: A CIA Directorate of Intelligence report discussed Indonesia’s proposed Fifth Force and Sukarno’s health as possible context but said confirming evidence for the speculation was lacking. Record this as an intelligence hypothesis, not a proven cause of G30S. [S1316;C2319]

1967 July 1967–January 1968, East Java: Leksana’s Brawijaya-archive reconstruction reports a Java-wide commanders’ meeting in July, a provincial New Order guidance-team decree in September, a territorial chain of Korem/Kodim teams, replacement of village heads by military caretakers in former PKI areas and postponement of local elections. Original 316-b orders and local implementation records remain uninspected. [S1410;C2585;CV612]

1967 1967–1970: Former aide Sidarto and Sukarno's son Guruh later described movement restrictions and family visits during Sukarno's restricted residence. Their memories are evidence of how two close witnesses recalled access controls, not a substitute for permits, guard orders or visitor logs. [S615–S616; C1037–C1038]

1967 After 1967: Sukarno's isolation, death, medical care, posthumous memory and later political rehabilitation remain only partly researched. Some family and aide testimony is now read; clinical, guard, visitor and correspondence records remain open. No stopping date is imposed. (Coverage CV019, CV339.)

1966 1966-12 to 1968-09: Following Sudisman’s arrest, Hearman’s oral-history reconstruction identifies an emergency Central Committee under Ruslan Wijayasastra, a Rewang-linked second-echelon network, and the South Blitar base. She describes Trisula as a military counterinsurgency that began in June 1968 and formally ended 7 September; village-level orders and party rosters remain missing. [S1310;C2293–C2300] Liem’s interviews add that armed-struggle start remained undecided, Kompro preparations and some cells were under construction, and some raids allegedly exceeded committee decisions. His chronology differentiates mid-1967 intelligence, a 20 February 1968 order, 5 May task-force formation and military-reported effective attacks from May to July; retain these stages alongside Hearman’s June start pending original orders. [S1317;C2321–C2328;C2337] Rewang also recalled that shelters were unfinished and that some local arms seizures/attacks exceeded the committee program; he retrospectively linked impatience and “shortcuts” to the military’s discovery and encirclement. Liem cites a military-history count of 234 ABRI caretakers and records a farmer’s account of being ordered to carry supplies for a mapping patrol. These are different source chains and remain unverified against the 1969 book and local rosters. [S1317;C2561–C2562;C2685–C2687]

1967 1967–1968, East Java official chronology and administrative phases: The government-sponsored 1978 Sejarah Daerah Jawa Timur reports 144 ABRI caretaker village officials (Liem’s indirect 1969 military-history count is 234), a 20 May 1968 civil-territorial post at Bakung with education/food/infrastructure duties, a 31 May Trisula start, and separate territorial phases through 7 September and 16 December. It also repeats military estimates of two Detga, 17 Gerda and 850 detainees. These figures and allegations remain attributed to the official narrative; its cited military history and 1975 regency booklet are not in hand. It cites a 10 November 1968 “Pedoman Aksi Kota” as evidence for a March 1968 plan, a chronology that requires the original document before it can support pre-operation intent. [S1390;S1393;S1437;C2688–C2695]

1968 1968-02-17 / 1968-02-28, East Java and the U.S. Embassy in Jakarta: An Indonesian Army General Staff I/SUAD-I report dated 17 February, based on captive interrogations and captured papers according to its own text, described Kompro/Triko structures and attributed a South Blitar Kompro roster to captive Subandi. It said an intended general conflict for early 1968 had not begun for lack of readiness reports from other regions; Rewang later said no start date had been set and that provincial committees had to approve any campaign. The two accounts conflict. The U.S. Embassy cover sheet says it received the translation through Husein Ahmaduddin and explicitly cautions it had no collateral evidence that the reported “revenge killings” were the work of a cohesive PKI group. Neither the interrogation-derived allegations nor the later participant recollection substitute for original minutes, captured documents, local incident records or interrogation files. [S1487;S1317;C2821–C2828]

1968 June–July 1968, South Blitar: Hearman describes village evacuations and Bakung holding camps used to obstruct food reaching fugitives; a later oral-history account by the pseudonymous Zainab says camp residents had to bring their own supplies despite promised kitchens/medical care and were hungry. She also records post-return food/property loss and daily military reporting/household work. Liem’s separate 2001 interviews with Paijo and Maryono both describe the killing and forced burial of roughly forty detainees; Maryono recalls eight put in a cave and thirty-two buried in two pits. These are retrospective witness accounts, not an audited casualty list or proof that every village had the same experience. [S1317;S1324;C2514–C2516;C2673;C2678–C2680]

1965 1965–1969 regional comparison: A 1968 U.S. NIE estimated very small post-purge PKI guerrilla groups relative to longer-standing CPM, CPB, CPT and Huk formations. These are contemporary intelligence estimates, not party censuses; the newer Philippine CPP/NPA formations belong to 1968–1969 and must not be conflated with the older PKP/Huk. [S1312–S1315;C2301–C2307] Additional bounded comparisons: the ECCC guide dates Cambodian armed-line adoption to 1965–66 and a CPK attack on police posts to 17 January 1968; Zeller stresses Vietnamese cadres and forces in Laos; Baird documents Hmong recruitment, training and limited central representation in the CPT. These are different organizations and source types, not a symmetrical regional dataset. [S1319–S1321;C2342–C2344]

Evidence-handling rules for this chronology

1. A contemporary claim is attributed to its authoring institution, date and information position.

2. Distinguish event, actor's intention, authorization, execution, delivery and effect.

3. Preserve competing dates, counts and names until primary documents or better research resolve them.

4. Every claim should point to source-register IDs and be broken into atomic claims in claim-ledger.csv.

5. The chronology is descriptive. Political judgments remain the author's decision after reviewing the record.
