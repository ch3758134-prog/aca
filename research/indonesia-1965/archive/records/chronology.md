# Working chronology: Indonesia, Sukarno and the road to 1965

**Status:** rolling research draft, not a finished narrative. No earliest year is imposed: earlier history must be added where evidence shows it is needed. Dates and labels are kept close to what the cited source actually establishes. A claim attributed to a government or intelligence service remains that actor's claim unless independently corroborated.

Confidence guide: **High** = directly recorded or independently corroborated basic date/action; **Moderate** = credible but source-limited, mediated or contested; **Open** = only a lead or preliminary claim.

## Before and during the colonial period

- **Before 1901 — not yet investigated.** Dutch colonial land, labor, education, racial/legal classification, regional administration and Islamic/nationalist networks may be necessary for explaining later class relations and political institutions. No historical starting boundary is set. (Coverage CV001.)

## Sukarno's formation and anti-colonial politics

- **6 June 1901:** ANRI and Cambridge's biographical summary place Sukarno's birth in Surabaya. They describe his father as Javanese and mother as Balinese. **Confidence: high for the broad facts; civil/family records not yet inspected.** [S001, S002]
- **c.1915–early 1920s:** ANRI's timeline says Sukarno attended school in Surabaya and lived in H.O.S. Tjokroaminoto's household; Cambridge identifies the Surabaya nationalist network and marriage to Tjokroaminoto's daughter, Siti Oetari. This household/political/personal connection is a key lead, not a complete account of patronage or family relations. [S001, S002]
- **1920/1921:** Sources disagree on the year Sukarno married Siti Oetari. ANRI's timeline itself records another source's year; Cambridge gives 1921. **Do not silently normalize the discrepancy.** [S001, S002]
- **1922/1923:** Cambridge dates Sukarno's marriage to Inggit Garnasih to 1922; ANRI's timeline records 24 April 1923 and cites a marriage certificate number. Underlying certificate and divorce records should resolve the conflict. [S001, S002]
- **1925–1927:** Cambridge lists the Bandung General Study Club (1925) and PNI founding (1927); ANRI dates PNI's foundation to 4 July 1927 and names associates. Party founding records, police surveillance and contemporary newspapers remain to be examined. [S001, S002]
- **1929–1938:** Cambridge summarizes Sukarno's arrest/trial and imprisonment through 1931, rearrest in 1933, exile to Flores and transfer to Bengkulu in 1938. Verify with court, prison, exile and correspondence records. [S001, S002]
- **1920s–1942:** Wider nationalist, socialist, Islamic, communist and labor networks, as well as Sukarno's intellectual influences and family/private relations, remain only partially covered. (Coverage CV002–CV003, CV020.)

## Japanese occupation and independence

- **1942:** Japanese authorities released Sukarno from Dutch exile/internment and used him in occupation-era political bodies. Cambridge confirms his leadership in Japanese-sponsored bodies; the exact institutions and dates require Japanese and Indonesian records. [S001]
- **1942–1945:** Sukarno's participation in occupation-sponsored organizations and mass mobilization formed part of both nationalist politics and Japan's war economy. The position involved agency and political responsibility while operating under coercive occupation; the extent and consequences must be investigated rather than assumed. Romusha labor, famine, PETA and recruitment into Japanese structures are major research lines. [S001, S016, S017]
- **1944–1945:** Later historiography debates Java famine mortality and how postwar claims, public memory and political actors represented it. Estimates have incentives and uncertain data; do not lift headline totals without methods. [S016]
- **17 August 1945:** Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed independence two days after Japan's surrender. The exact timing, youth pressure, Japanese restrictions and drafting of the proclamation require comparison of the text, BPUPKI/PPKI minutes, Japanese records, contemporary press and recollections. The Indonesian State Secretariat page was surfaced but not fully extractable in this pass. [S001, S006]

## Revolution and the Madiun conflict

- **1945–1946:** British forces under Allied authority, rather than U.S. forces, took responsibility for Japanese surrender in most of Indonesia and faced the return of Dutch forces. A U.S. retrospective describes this command arrangement and British contact with Republican officials; British, Dutch and Indonesian records are still needed. [S012]
- **1946–1947:** U.S. records describe recognition of the Republic's de facto authority under the Linggadjati framework, participation in mediation, and attempts to prevent a breakdown. These records are the U.S. government's account of its own policy. [S012]
- **1947–1948:** The United States worked through cease-fire, Good Offices Committee and Renville processes; it also discussed possible economic assistance to an interim arrangement. Policy mixed mediation, economic interests, anti-communism and alliance concerns. [S012]
- **18–20 September 1948:** Early U.S. telegrams report the Madiun takeover/armed conflict and reproduce Sukarno's broadcast and the Republic's statement. Some information is relayed via listeners, press agencies or Republican officials. Treat the words “coup,” “revolt,” and “affair” as source-specific labels, not neutral findings. [S007, S008]
- **September–December 1948:** Republican forces suppressed the Madiun movement; leading figures including Musso and Amir Sjarifuddin were killed or captured, according to later scholarly summaries. Exact sequence, command responsibility, casualty counts, executions and political intention remain to be reconstructed from Indonesian, military, party, Dutch and regional records. [S010, S015]
- **Interpretation remains contested:** Scholarship has challenged both a simple foreign-directed-coup account and a purely spontaneous/domestic account. Army reorganization, factional rivalry, breakdown of negotiations and Musso's return all appear in the literature. [S013–S015, S058]
- **18 December 1948–1949:** After the Dutch second military action, U.S. officials advocated Security Council pressure and used the prospect of economic aid to the Netherlands as leverage; an official later retrospective says U.S. support shifted toward Indonesian sovereignty in part because of Cold War calculations and Dutch military action. This sequence complicates a one-line account of U.S. policy. [S011, S012]
- **27 December 1949:** U.S. retrospective records the Round Table Conference agreement as the basis for transfer of sovereignty. Dutch, Indonesian, UN and federal-state perspectives must be added. [S012]

## Parliamentary state, Guided Democracy and foreign conflict

- **1950–1957:** Parliamentary governments, regional rebellions, the 1955 elections, Sukarno's personal authority, army political/economic power and party growth need a dedicated chronological tranche. The current archive has only incomplete official election result leads and broad biography summaries. (Coverage CV007.)
- **1955 elections:** Search-indexed KPU results distinguish the DPR election from the Constituent Assembly election; the PKI totals differ slightly. These numbers remain provisional until printed official result tables are checked. [S035]
- **1957–1958:** U.S. official records acknowledge covert support for regional dissidents, including approved political action, military assistance and air support; CAT pilots volunteered and flew combat missions. A separate April 1958 U.S.–UK memo discusses options, including covert-only action and hypothetical intervention. Documented activity and merely discussed options must remain separate. [S018–S020]
- **5 July 1959:** Decree No. 150 dissolved the Constituent Assembly and restored the 1945 Constitution. The text is primary legal evidence for what the decree ordered, not a complete answer to its legitimacy or political effects. [S022, S036]
- **1958–1965:** Dutch enterprise nationalization, Indonesian-Soviet military and economic ties, Chinese-Indonesian relations, Japanese reparations, U.S. policy and British/Australian regional operations form separate foreign-intervention strands. Evidence levels are highly uneven; see the intervention matrix. [S045–S051, S056]
- **1960:** Indonesia enacted a Basic Agrarian Law and a sharecropping law; land ceilings were also legislated. Their implementation, uneven enforcement, local land conflict and party/union actions are not inferable from enactment alone. [S037–S039, S041]
- **1960–1965:** PKI and affiliated mass organizations expanded, but membership estimates are not census counts and each organization had distinct membership, autonomy and local relations. Need an Indonesian party/union archive and regional case studies. [S040, S053]
- **1964–1965:** A February 1965 U.S. 303 Committee progress report describes political action against the PKI, propaganda and liaison/support; some activities were underway while an additional program awaited approval. The agency is partly redacted. [S024]
- **20 July 1965:** A U.S. record discusses a commercial license for communications equipment requested by the Indonesian Army; licensing is not proof of delivery or use. [S025]

## 30 September Movement, state violence and transfer of power

- **30 September–1 October 1965:** A U.S. intelligence document dated 6 October identifies Untung, records Suharto as Kostrad commander assuming Army command, and advances an early leftist-coup hypothesis. It is evidence of what U.S. analysts believed at that date, not proof of PKI command or Sukarno's responsibility. [S026]
- **October 1965:** One U.S. memorandum's statement that U.S. involvement had been limited to quiet assurances of non-interference is narrowly scoped; the same documentary record documents earlier covert action and later assistance. [S023–S027]
- **November 1965–June 1966:** U.S. documents record a covert procurement path for medical supplies and a communications system for anti-communist Army leaders. A follow-up reports partial assembly/use, not delivery of the entire network. Requests, approvals, purchases, shipments, receipt and effect require separate evidence. [S029–S033]
- **December 1965 onward:** U.S. records document transfers of lists of suspected PKI and affiliated-organization members to Indonesian authorities. This establishes transfer, not the causal role of any particular name in a detention or killing. [S032]
- **1965–1966 killings and mass arrests:** Scholarly estimates support a very large national death toll—commonly several hundred thousand—with substantial uncertainty and regional variation. Current review is abstract-level; exact totals, locations, state/Army direction, civilian participation and the timing of violence require local records and full methodological review. [S042–S044]
- **11 March 1966–1967:** Supersemar document versions, chain of custody, subsequent orders and the legal transfer of authority remain unresolved here. The MPR database returned 403 and a repository PDF timed out. No authenticity verdict has been entered. [S054, S055]
- **After 1967:** Sukarno's isolation, death, posthumous memory and later political rehabilitation have not yet been researched. No stopping date is imposed. (Coverage CV019.)

## Evidence-handling rules for this chronology

1. A contemporary claim is attributed to its authoring institution, date and information position.
2. Distinguish event, actor's intention, authorization, execution, delivery and effect.
3. Preserve competing dates, counts and names until primary documents or better research resolve them.
4. Every claim should point to source-register IDs and be broken into atomic claims in `claim-ledger.csv`.
5. The chronology is descriptive. Political judgments remain the author's decision after reviewing the record.
